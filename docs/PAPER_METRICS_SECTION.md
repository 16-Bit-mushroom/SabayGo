## 2.3.2.2 Evaluation of the Passenger Headcount Model

The headcount pipeline employs a pretrained YOLOv8n checkpoint rather than a
model trained for this study, as stated in Section 2.3.2.1. The evaluation
therefore characterises the detector's counting accuracy at the system's
configured operating point, and the consequences of that accuracy for the
reconciliation rule, rather than reporting training performance for which no
training was performed. Because the pipeline's output is a passenger count
and its claim concerns the variance between two counts, count-level ground
truth measures the quantity of interest directly; detection-level overlap
metrics such as mean Average Precision were not computed, as they grade an
intermediate representation the reconciliation logic discards.

**Evaluation data.** The model was evaluated on the COCO val2017 validation
split, which is held out from the COCO train2017 partition on which the
deployed checkpoint was trained. The split provides per-instance human
annotations, from which an exact ground-truth passenger count is obtained
without additional manual labelling. Images were filtered by three criteria
intended to preserve the integrity of the ground truth and the relevance of
the sample to a van cabin: images containing `iscrowd` regions were excluded,
because such regions denote an indeterminate number of persons; images were
restricted to between one and fourteen annotated persons, matching the
capacity constraint of a UV Express unit; and images in which any annotated
person occupied less than one percent of the frame area were excluded, as a
seated cabin occupant subtends a substantially larger proportion of the frame
than a distant pedestrian. Fifty images containing no annotated persons were
retained to measure the false-positive rate on an unoccupied cabin. Table A
summarises the resulting composition.

**Table A. Composition of the evaluation set**

**Counting accuracy.** Table B reports the error between the detected count
C_visual and the annotated count, at the deployed confidence threshold of
0.45. Across the full set the model attains a mean absolute error of 0.34
passengers, with 74.7 percent of frames counted exactly and 94.3 percent
within one passenger. Disaggregating by occupancy, however, reveals that this
aggregate is dominated by sparsely occupied frames. Accuracy declines
monotonically as occupancy rises, and the mean signed error is negative
throughout and increases in magnitude, indicating systematic undercounting
rather than symmetric noise. At eight to eleven occupants the model
underestimates by 2.46 passengers on average. This behaviour is consistent
with inter-occupant occlusion: in a densely occupied cabin, passengers
physically obscure one another from a single camera position, and an occluded
occupant cannot be detected.

The unoccupied row is of independent interest: across fifty frames
containing no persons, the model returned a count of zero in every case,
indicating that spurious detection on an empty cabin is not a contributing
failure mode at this threshold.

**Reconciliation performance.** Because the reconciliation rule compares
C_visual against the manifested count C_booked, the count error translates
directly into the rule's operating characteristics. For an evaluated frame
with ground-truth occupancy C_true, a manifest concealing k boardings is
simulated as C_booked = C_true − k, giving Δ = C_visual − C_booked. At k = 0
no leakage is present and any alert is a false alarm raised against a
compliant crew; at k ≥ 1 an alert constitutes correct detection. Table C
reports both alert rules appearing in the preceding sections: the strict rule
Δ ≠ 0 of the reconciliation formula, and the directional rule Δ > 0
corresponding to a physical count exceeding the manifest.

**Table C. Reconciliation performance by cabin occupancy (threshold 0.45)**

| Passengers present | Images | False alarm, Δ ≠ 0 (%) | False alarm, Δ > 0 (%) | Detects 1 concealed (%) ᵇ | Detects 2 concealed (%) ᵇ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (unoccupied) | 50 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| 1–3 | 1,424 | 21.7 | 2.7 | 81.0 | 97.3 |
| 4–7 | 139 | 56.8 | 2.2 | 45.3 | 76.3 |
| 8–11 | 28 | 89.3 | 7.1 | 17.9 | 35.7 |
| **All** | **1,643** | **25.3** | **2.7** | **77.5** | **94.6** |

ᵇ Detection rates reported under the directional rule Δ > 0.

The two rules exhibit markedly different characteristics. Pooled across the
set, the strict rule raises an alert on 25.3 percent of compliant trips,
against 2.7 percent for the directional rule; the disparity arises because
the strict rule also fires on undercounting, which Table B shows to be the
model's dominant error. The directional rule is correspondingly conservative,
detecting a single concealed boarding in 77.5 percent of pooled cases. Both
rules degrade as occupancy increases, in opposing directions: at eight to
eleven occupants the strict rule raises a false alarm on 89.3 percent of
compliant trips, while the directional rule, though quiet, detects only 17.9
percent of single concealed boardings. The common cause is the systematic
undercount reported in Table B, which at high occupancy is numerically
indistinguishable from a concealed boarding.

A specific failure mode warrants explicit statement. Under the strict rule,
concealment of exactly k boardings escapes detection precisely when the
detector underestimates by exactly k, as the two errors cancel and Δ
evaluates to zero. Across the evaluated set this coincidence occurred in 17.1
percent of cases for k = 1 and 3.8 percent for k = 2.

**Threshold sensitivity.** The confidence threshold governing which
detections are counted was varied across five values, with results in
Table D. Reducing the threshold recovers detections the model would otherwise
discard, lowering the undercount rate from 22.5 percent at 0.45 to 12.3
percent at 0.25; however, not all recovered detections correspond to real
occupants, and the overcount rate rises correspondingly from 2.7 to 9.6
percent. The two alert rules are therefore affected in opposite directions.
The strict rule Δ ≠ 0, which fires on errors of either sign, improves
modestly at lower thresholds because undercounting is the larger component.
The directional rule Δ > 0 fires only on overcounting, so its false-alarm
rate — the rate at which a compliant crew is wrongly implicated — rises
monotonically as the threshold falls, from 0.7 percent at 0.65 to 9.6 percent
at 0.25, while its detection rate rises in step.

Under the directional rule, threshold selection is therefore a policy
judgement about the relative cost of a missed concealment and a false
accusation, rather than a quantity the evaluation can determine. The deployed
value of 0.45 yields a 2.7 percent false-accusation rate at 77.5 percent
detection of a single concealed boarding; 0.35 yields 4.6 percent at 82.8
percent.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.28 | 78.1 | 9.6 | 12.3 | 21.9 | 9.6 | 87.7 |
| 0.35 | 0.28 | 78.2 | 4.6 | 17.2 | 21.8 | 4.6 | 82.8 |
| 0.45 ᶜ | 0.34 | 74.7 | 2.7 | 22.5 | 25.3 | 2.7 | 77.5 |
| 0.55 | 0.43 | 70.7 | 1.3 | 28.0 | 29.3 | 1.3 | 72.0 |
| 0.65 | 0.55 | 64.9 | 0.7 | 34.4 | 35.1 | 0.7 | 65.6 |

ᶜ Deployed value.  ᵈ Reported under the directional rule Δ > 0.

The reasoning originally offered for the deployed value — that a dimly
illuminated cabin would produce spurious low-confidence detections requiring
suppression — is not supported by the degradation results reported below.
Reducing frame brightness to 60 and 35 percent of nominal did not increase
the overcount rate, which fell slightly from 2.7 percent to 2.1 and 1.9
percent respectively, and produced no false detection on any of the fifty
unoccupied frames under either condition. Attenuated illumination causes this
detector to register fewer objects, not spurious ones. The deployed threshold
remains defensible under the directional rule on the trade-off above, but not
on the premise originally stated.

**Robustness to degraded capture conditions.** To characterise sensitivity to
the capture conditions of an operational cabin, the evaluation set was
reprocessed under six controlled degradations, with results in Table E.
Degradation increases error modestly and in a single direction: the mean
signed error remains negative throughout and grows in magnitude, confirming
that adverse capture conditions cause this detector to miss occupants rather
than to register spurious ones. Reduction of spatial resolution to one
quarter, and horizontal motion blur, produced the largest degradation,
exceeding that of illumination reduction to 35 percent of nominal.

**Table E. Counting accuracy under degraded capture conditions (threshold 0.45)**

| Condition | MAE | RMSE | Mean signed error | Exact count (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unmodified | 0.34 | 0.79 | −0.28 | 74.7 |
| Illumination 60% | 0.34 | 0.81 | −0.30 | 75.0 |
| Illumination 35% | 0.38 | 0.86 | −0.34 | 73.3 |
| Resolution halved | 0.36 | 0.82 | −0.30 | 74.1 |
| Resolution quartered | 0.49 | 1.04 | −0.44 | 66.6 |
| Horizontal motion blur | 0.45 | 0.99 | −0.39 | 69.2 |
| JPEG re-encoding, quality 70 | 0.37 | 0.85 | −0.32 | 73.6 |

Each degradation was applied to the full set of 1,643 evaluation images.

**Inference latency.** Measured on the development host, single-frame
inference required a mean of 101 ms, a median of 91 ms, and a 95th percentile
of 140 ms across 1,643 frames, exclusive of image acquisition and network
transport. These figures were obtained on a general-purpose workstation and
do not characterise the Orange Pi 5 edge device specified in Section 2.3.4,
which was not available for measurement.

**Limitations of the evaluation.** The evaluation images are photographs of
general scenes satisfying occupancy and framing criteria chosen for
relevance, not footage acquired from a camera mounted within a UV Express
cabin, which was unavailable during development. Two consequences follow.
First, performance under the illumination, seat geometry, and camera
placement of an operational cabin is not established by these results.
Second, and more materially, the validation split affords limited coverage of
the high-occupancy regime: only twenty-eight evaluated images contain eight
or more occupants, and none contains fourteen. As Tables B and C show that
performance is strongly occupancy-dependent, the figures reported for a
densely occupied cabin rest on a small sample and should be treated as
indicative. Establishing performance at operational capacity requires imagery
acquired at known occupancy within a vehicle, which is identified as the
immediate next step for this component.
